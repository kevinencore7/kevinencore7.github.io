Telling the Bees

The story

After the funeral, Mara walked the long way home, past the orchard where old Ellery’s hives stood in a crooked row like luggage nobody had come back for. Bees, he had told her once, must be told everything: births, marriages, deaths, or they take offence and leave. “Customs,” she had said, and he had shrugged, as if custom were only another word for what works.

Dusk was coming on, and the hives hummed low, the sound a kettle makes before it decides to boil. Everyone in the village assumed his nephew would sell the land, and nobody had said a word to the bees. For a while she stood at the gate, feeling foolish, holding the black ribbon the undertaker had pinned to her coat. Good sense said go home.

Her feet went in anyway, down the wet grass between the apple trees, until she was close enough to see the guards turning at the entrances, and she knocked softly on the side of the first hive, as he always had, to get their attention. “I’m sorry,” she said to the first hive, and then, because that sounded like an apology for something she had done, “I mean, I’m sorry to tell you that he died on Tuesday.” Just that, and the hum did not change at all. Kneeling, she tied a strip of the ribbon to the handle of the first hive, Agnes (he had named them all, in white paint on the lids), the way he had shown her on her mother’s hives nine years ago. Later she would not be able to say why she went on to the second, and the third, and on down the row, telling each one the same small fact. Most of the bees ignored her; one walked across her knuckle and flew off, unhurried, as if to report.

No one had seen, she thought, until she heard the gate and turned to find the nephew, Tom, in his funeral suit, holding a torch. “Oh,” he said. “People said you’d be here,” he added, which was not true, because nobody had known she would be, least of all her. Quietly he came down the row and read the names in the torchlight, Agnes, Bramble, Little Joan, as if calling a register. “Right,” he said at the end, “he’d have liked that,” and his voice went somewhere for a moment and came back.

She asked him what he would do with the land. “Too soon to say,” he said, and then, after a while, “I don’t know the first thing about bees.” Unasked, she found herself saying that she did, a little, and that the first thing was that you talk to them. Very carefully, as if the hives might be listening, he asked whether she would show him. “Wednesday,” she said, “around noon, when most of them are out.” Xanthe, the last in the row and the only one painted yellow, was the hive he stopped at on the way out, and he told it himself, awkwardly, in four words.

Years later, when the orchard was his and the row was twice as long, he would still tell people that was the night he learned to keep bees, though all he had learned that night was the first thing. Zinnias grow along the fence now, planted every spring by the two of them, and the bees, who were told about that too, seem to approve.

The custom, checked

The story leans on an old custom: when someone in a household died, married or was born, the bees were told, or they might leave. I re-read the story a day after writing it and checked the custom against three sources:

  • Wikipedia, “Telling the bees”, a summary that cites 19th- and early-20th-century accounts from England, Ireland, Wales, several other European countries and parts of the United States.
  • John Greenleaf Whittier, “Telling the Bees” (1858), with his headnote on the custom in rural New England, from Representative Poetry Online.
  • The royal beekeeper John Chapple in September 2022, who told the palace hives of the Queen’s death and tied black ribbons on them, as quoted in the Jerusalem Post.

Telling the bees of births, marriages and deaths, the fear that they leave if they are not told, a black ribbon or a shred of black on each hive, knocking first, and telling each hive in turn are all in those sources. I invented the rest: the hives’ names, the undertaker’s ribbon, the handles and painted lids, and telling the bees about the zinnias, which the custom does not cover. In the custom the bees are told at once, usually by someone in the household; in the story a neighbour tells them late, because nobody else did.

The re-read also changed three sentences: she now knocks before she speaks, as the sources say keepers did; she offers to show him the hives around noon rather than in the morning, which is when beekeepers usually open them; and the second-last sentence no longer repeats a point the story had already made.
