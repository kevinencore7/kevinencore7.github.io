The sandpile that does nothing

Pour sand on a grid by a simple rule and the piles that keep coming back form a group. That group has an identity element: one particular pile that, added to any of the others, changes nothing. Nobody would guess what it looks like. Choose a grid size and your browser will work it out.

The rule

Each cell of an N × N grid holds some grains of sand. A cell with 4 or more grains topples: it loses 4 and gives one to each of its neighbours. A grain pushed off the edge of the grid is lost. Toppling can make a neighbour topple, and so on, but because sand leaves at the edge it always stops, with every cell holding 0 to 3 grains. The pile it stops at doesn't depend on the order in which cells topple. That is why the model is called Abelian, and it is why the picture above comes out the same however the program happens to sweep the grid.

Add two stable piles cell by cell and let them topple, and you have a way of combining piles. Restricted to the recurrent piles, the ones you keep seeing if you go on dropping sand at random forever, it makes a group. Its size is the number of spanning trees of the grid with the edge joined into one extra vertex, by the matrix-tree theorem. My own brute-force count for N = 1, 2 and 3 found 4, 192 and 100,352 recurrent piles, and those are the spanning-tree counts. computed

How the identity is found

Put 6 grains on every cell and let it topple; call the result stab(6). Take that away from 6 cell by cell, and let the difference topple. The result is the identity: e = stab(6 − stab(6)).

Why this works, in brief. Toppling never changes which class a pile is in, if two piles count as the same when they differ by a whole number of topplings. So 6 − stab(6) is in the same class as 6 − 6, the empty pile, which plays the part of zero. And 6 − stab(6) has at least 3 grains on every cell, which is enough to make what it topples down to recurrent. A recurrent pile in the class of zero is the identity. my reading

The page checks its own answer each time: it adds e to itself, lets it topple, and gets e back. computed

The square in the middle

At every size I have measured, from 10 to 800, the middle of the identity is a solid square of 2s. (At N = 1 there is none: the identity there is a single empty cell.) The page measures it the way my notes do: the largest square of 2s centred on the grid. For odd N a cross through the middle is left out of the test, because there the identity has a line of 1s along the centre row and column with a 0 where they meet. That is why the odd sizes come out one cell wider than the even size just below. computed

  • The square's existence isn't proved. As far as I know it is still a conjecture. A 2026 paper says the conjecture of Dhar and co-authors about it “remains open”, but I know that only from a summary of the paper, not from reading it. secondhand
  • The one published size. Dhar, Ruelle, Sen and Verma wrote in 1995 that on a 2L × 2L lattice the side of the central square “was numerically measured for L up to 100, and goes like 4L/5”, which is 0.40 N. read Their own small tables, for N = 10 and 11, match the identities computed here digit for digit. read computed
  • What I measure. Over even N from 64 to 800 (34 sizes), a straight line fitted by least squares gives a side of 0.4165 N + 0.65 cells, never more than 1.1 cells from the data. At every even N from 64 up, the side is within −0.5 to +1.67 cells of 5N/12 = 0.4167 N, and exactly 5N/12 at N = 120, 240, 384 and 600. At N = 200, 4L/5 would give 80 cells; the side is 84. computed
  • A fit, not a proof. The fitted slopes, 0.4165 to 0.4167 depending on where the fit starts (N = 64, 100 or 200), are within 0.0002 of 5/12 = 0.41667. That doesn't make it the limit. The side wobbles by about a cell from size to size, and nothing I have shows why the slope should be 5/12 rather than something close to it. my reading
  • I read the 4/5 as a rough fit to smaller sizes rather than a measure of something else, because the same passage gives the square's area as about 4/25 of the lattice. my reading
Again · 24 Nov 2026

0.42 was a coincidence

My first pass measured only N = 50, 100, 200 and 400. At 100, 200 and 400 the square is exactly 0.42 N (42, 84, 168), doubling when N doubles, and I guessed the limit was near 0.42. A second look at 73 sizes up to 800 showed that was a coincidence of those sizes: the side wobbles by about a cell around 5N/12, and exact doubling fails elsewhere (28, 54, 108 at N = 64, 128, 256, not 28, 56, 112). The measurements above are from that second look. computed

What to try

  • N = 10 and 11. Small enough to count cells. The even grid has a 4 × 4 square of 2s; the odd one is 5 wide with its cross of 1s. computed
  • N = 100. The square is 42 cells, 0.42 N. computed
  • N = 150. 64 cells, 0.4267 N. At N = 140 it was 0.4143 N, the widest and narrowest of the even sizes I measured from 100 up. computed
  • Watch it form. The grey is sand still toppling. Most of the work is the first step, letting 6 on every cell settle: at N = 200 that takes 211 million topplings, and the second step 78 million. computed

How the page works

  • The script uses the same rule and the same sweep order as the C program behind my notes. At N = 10, 11, 50 and 100 its identity is the same as that program's cell for cell, it counts the same number of topplings, and its square's side is the one in my measurement table (4, 5, 22 and 42). computed
  • The work grows like N⁴: about N² cells, each toppling about N² times, roughly 0.18 N⁴ topplings in all. N = 160 takes 119 million. The page stops at 160: in my tests one run there took about a second, and about four with the processor slowed fourfold, as a stand-in for a phone. At 200 it took two seconds and nine.
