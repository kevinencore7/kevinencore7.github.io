Why the sundial is sixteen minutes fast

On 3 November a sundial is more than sixteen minutes ahead of the clock; in February it is fourteen minutes behind. Photograph the Sun at the same clock time every day for a year and it traces a lopsided figure 8. Both come from two causes, the shape of the Earth's orbit and the tilt of its axis. Here you can turn each one off.

What is being measured

A sundial shows apparent solar time: noon is when the Sun crosses the meridian. A clock shows mean time, which is what the Sun would show if it moved at an even pace along the equator. The difference, sundial minus clock, is called the equation of time. Here it is positive when the sundial is fast, as in November.

The Sun's place along its yearly path, the ecliptic, is its longitude λ; its place along the equator is its right ascension α. The imagined even-paced sun is at the mean longitude L₀. Then the equation of time is L₀ − α, and it splits exactly into two parts. computed

  • L₀ − λ, from the eccentricity. The orbit is an ellipse, and by Kepler's second law the Earth moves fastest near perihelion, in early January. So the Sun runs ahead of and behind its average place once a year.
  • λ − α, from the tilt. Even an evenly moving Sun on the tilted ecliptic would not move evenly in right ascension, the coordinate a clock measures. Near the equinoxes its path slants across the hour lines; near the solstices it runs along them, where they are closer together. That makes a wobble twice a year.

The nutation and aberration left over are under a tenth of a second all year. computed

Why sixteen minutes, and why not the same both ways

Both parts are positive in early November: the tilt term peaks at +9m52s on 8 November, and the eccentricity term is still near its +7m39s peak of 5 October. On 3 November they add to sixteen and a half minutes. In February the tilt term is at its lowest, but the eccentricity term is only about −3.7 to −5 minutes, so the sundial is at most fourteen minutes slow. In May and July the two terms pull against each other, which gives the two small turning points. computed my reading

On the sliders: with the orbit made circular, the curve is the tilt term alone, ±9m52s, symmetric, twice a year. With the tilt taken away, it is the eccentricity term alone, ±7m39s, once a year. computed Those two curves don't simply add up to the real one: they differ from it by up to 37 seconds, because in the real sky the tilt acts on the Sun's true place, not its average place. The split above avoids that by taking λ − α at the true λ. computed

Why a figure 8

The analemma plots the Sun's height in the sky (its declination) against the equation of time, at the same clock time every day. Declination goes up and down once a year. The tilt term goes back and forth twice a year. One against two makes a figure 8, the 1 : 2 Lissajous figure; with a circular orbit the 8 is symmetric and crosses itself at the equator. The eccentricity term, once a year, then pushes the curve sideways, which moves the crossing up and makes the lobes unequal. With no tilt at all the declination never changes and the 8 flattens into a horizontal line. computed my reading

The southern lobe is the big one because perihelion, about 3 January, falls near the December solstice. Around the bottom of the 8 the eccentricity term and the tilt term push in the same direction and spread that lobe sideways; in the northern summer they partly cancel. my reading Move the perihelion slider to June and the big lobe moves to the top.

Flamsteed, 1672

John Flamsteed's De inaequalitate dierum solarium was printed in 1673 with Jeremiah Horrocks's posthumous works. He says the inequality of days comes from two causes, "vel ab excentricitate Solis … vel ab inclinatione Axis Terrae", from the Sun's eccentricity or from the tilt of the Earth's axis. Astronomers accepted the second, he says, but the first was, in my translation, "either rejected altogether, or misused". That is the split above. read

  • His eccentricity table is an exact Kepler ellipse: all 183 legible entries fit one with e = 0.017320 to 0.28 s rms. That e is 2.8% larger than the modern value for 1670, 0.016846, so his eccentricity term is about 13 seconds too big at its peak. computed One entry is wrong: at zero anomaly the table prints 0′03″ where a Kepler orbit gives 0. Its neighbours, 8, 16 and 24 seconds, fit a curve through zero. read computed
  • His tilt table matches λ − α computed with his own obliquity, 23°30′, to under half an arcsecond rms. His obliquity is 64″ too large, which costs under a second. computed
  • His worked example for 19 August 1646 reproduces at every printed step. Against modern elements his answer, 12 seconds, is about 10 seconds off, almost all of it from the oversized eccentricity. computed
  • Street's "Caroline" tables, he says, are wrong by fifteen minutes and sometimes more all through March. They get the eccentricity term's sign reversed, which makes the error twice that term: 15.2 to 15.9 minutes. computed
  • Added on re-check: His synopsis table is dated 1167, and it isn't a misprint for 1667: the Sun's places match the twelfth century, and he calls them examples from a more remote century. He says the tables that use the tilt alone are wrong by at most 7′49″, but by his own columns the difference reaches 7′55″ on 10 and 15 March, so 7′49″ is the value on 5 March, not the largest. read computed

So Flamsteed's account has the right structure, two causes with a table for each, added with signs, and arithmetic exact to the printed digit. His remaining error, about 13 seconds at most, comes from one input, an eccentricity a little too large. my reading

How the page works

  • The script recomputes the year whenever a slider moves. The model: the Sun's elements from Meeus's polynomials, Kepler's equation solved exactly, four terms of nutation and the aberration. Against Horizons over 2026 it is off by 1.0 s rms and 2.05 s at worst. For comparison, NOAA's published Fourier formula is off by 23.4 s rms, and the two-term formula −2e sin M + tan²(ε/2) sin 2L₀ (in radians, with M the mean anomaly) by 22.2 s. computed
  • The script is a port of the Python model behind my note. For every day of 2026, and with each cause switched off, it agrees with that program to better than a microsecond, and with its printed table of 2026 values to the hundredth of a second it prints. computed
  • The sliders scale the real elements. At their starting values the eccentricity and tilt are the real ones for each day, which drift very slightly through the year; the figures shown are for 2 July 2026. Moving the perihelion slider rotates the orbit's long axis while keeping the calendar fixed to the seasons.
  • Turning points and zeros are found on the continuous model, not just at daily samples; the crossing of the 8 is found between daily points, as in the note.
