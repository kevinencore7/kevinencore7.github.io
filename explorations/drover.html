Drover

A small game I made up, for two players on a 5 × 5 board, in which you never move your own piece, only your opponent's. Here you play it against a computer that plays perfectly. Pick a side.

The rules, in short

  • Two pieces, X and O, on a 5 × 5 board. X moves first.
  • On your turn you move your opponent's piece one step in any of the 8 directions, like a chess king, onto a cell that is not burned and not the other piece's.
  • The cell it left is burned for the rest of the game. Nothing may enter it.
  • If at the start of your turn your opponent's piece has nowhere to go, it is stranded and you win. There are no draws: every move burns a cell, so a game lasts at most 23 moves.

So each of you draws the other's trail, and every trail is a wall for both pieces. The walls around your own piece are mostly ones you drew yourself. A move is written as the cell the moved piece went to.

The first rules start X on c1 and O on c5. The corner start, a1 and e1, is the same game from other cells. It is the default here, for the reasons below. I don't know of a published game with exactly these rules, but I haven't searched thoroughly, so I'm not claiming it is new.

Who wins

From both starts, X wins with perfect play. So if you let the computer play X, it cannot lose, and every game you play as O will end with your piece stranded. As X you can win, but only by finding the right moves. computed

  • Corner start: 2 of X's 3 first moves win (d1 and d2). With best play on both sides the game lasts 14 moves, and X's 5th and 7th moves each have only one right answer, out of 4 and out of 5. computed
  • First rules: 4 of X's 5 first moves win (b4, d4, b5, d5; only c4 loses). The game lasts 8 moves with best play, and X has at least two winning moves on every turn of the line below, so the win is easy to keep. computed

Why the corner start is the default: the game lasts almost twice as long, the winner has to keep finding exact moves, and the loser has 2 to 6 moves to choose from on each turn of the line above, so a defence takes work. It is still a first-player win: every finite game without draws is a win for one side or the other, and here, as from the first rules, that side is X. What changes is how much happens in the middle. my reading

When the computer is winning it plays a winning move. When it is losing, it plays the move that leaves you the fewest winning replies, so you have to find them. It doesn't try to win quickly or to lose slowly, so a game against it can be longer or shorter than the perfect lines above.

How the page works

  • The script is a port of the Python program I wrote the game with: the same rules, the same search (it remembers every position it has solved and tries first the moves that leave the other piece fewest exits), and the same choice of move. On 2,000 random positions from games played at random, 1,000 from each start, the two programs gave the same legal moves, the same win or loss for the player to move and the same computer move every time. computed
  • Solving the corner start, with all of X's first moves, takes the script 402,818 positions, the same count as the Python program. The solving happens when the computer first has to move, and is remembered after that, so later moves are instant. When the computer plays X it stops at the first winning move it finds, so its first move needs 227,905 positions, not all 402,818; that took about 0.4 s in my tests, and about 1.4 s with the processor slowed fourfold, as a stand-in for a phone. So the page solves the game in your browser rather than loading a stored answer. computed
