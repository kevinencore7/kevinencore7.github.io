Méchain's error and the metre

In the 1790s Delambre and Méchain measured the meridian from Dunkirk to Barcelona to define the metre, and at Barcelona Méchain hid a discrepancy in his latitudes. The popular story is that this, or a wrong figure for the Earth's flattening, made the metre short. Delambre's own volumes say otherwise.

In brief

  • The discrepancy was 3.24″ of latitude, about 100 m on the ground, and Delambre printed it himself in 1810.
  • It did not affect the metre as adopted. The 1799 commission never saw the Barcelona observations and used the other station, Montjouy, alone.
  • Had it been used, the metre would have been longer by 0.046 mm (taking the mean of the two stations) or 0.093 mm (taking Barcelona alone). Both go towards the modern value.
  • The assumed flattening of the Earth (1/334 instead of today's 1/298.26) is worth only about 0.02 mm, and with the opposite sign: it made the metre slightly longer.
  • The legal metre is 0.197 mm short of a ten-millionth of the quadrant. Essentially all of that comes from the plumb line at the ends of the arc being pulled off vertical, chiefly by about 10″ at Montjouy: three times Méchain's 3.24″.

What Delambre printed

The latitudes used. For the whole arc Delambre takes Dunkirk at 51° 2′ 9.2″ and Montjouy at 41° 21′ 44.96″, a difference of 9° 40′ 24.24″, against a measured arc of 551,583.6 toises (vol. III, pp. 77 and 101). read

The discrepancy, stated outright (vol. III, pp. 110–111): read

"Dans l'incertitude où nous sommes sur la cause qui a pu produire la différence de 3″24 entre deux latitudes déterminées avec les mêmes instrumens, les mêmes soins et le même accord, par un des observateurs les plus habiles que nous connoissions, nous ne pouvons prendre d'autre parti que [le] moyen arithmétique."

That is: not knowing what caused a 3.24″ difference between two latitudes taken with the same instruments and care by one of the ablest observers he knew, he can only take the arithmetic mean. For one sub-arc he does exactly that, 41° 21′ 46.58″, which is 44.96″ plus half of 3.24″. read computed

The commission never saw it. The table of contents of vol. III: "La Commission n'avoit pas connoissance des observations de Barcelone … On en a conclu le mètre de 443.295936 lignes." read

His verdict in 1810. The metre "est de 443.32, à fort peu près", against the 443.296 adopted, and "la latitude de Barcelone paroît meilleure que celle de Montjouy" (p. 557). He attributes the trouble to irregularities of the Earth, "prouvé par les observations de M. Méchain à Barcelone et à Montjouy". read

my reading So the number was not hidden from the published record for long. Delambre printed it, with his doubts and his method, eleven years after the commission and six after Méchain's death. What was hidden was the Barcelona series from the 1799 commission and, according to Alder, Méchain's later reworking of his notebooks. secondhand

The story in the secondary sources

Over the winter of 1793–94 Méchain measured his inn's latitude (the Fontana de Oro, in Barcelona) from three stars, then got one day at the fort of Montjouy to tie the two stations together. They disagreed by about 3.2″. He told no one. After his death Delambre found that later versions of the notebooks had moved the Fontana de Oro values by about 3″. A later re-analysis by Nicollet, combining stars north and south of the zenith at each site, made the two stations agree to about 0.25″: the "error" looks like an instrumental or refraction effect that pushes north and south stars opposite ways. secondhand

The stations are about 884 toises apart 1,094 toises apart (vol. II, p. 565), 950 toises in the meridian, so 100 m is about 5% of their separation, and 0.01% of the whole arc. read computed

my reading On these inputs the plumb line is the whole story: about 0.20 mm against a 0.197 mm shortfall. The flattening matters little because the arc is centred near 45°, where a degree of latitude is close to a ninetieth of the quadrant for any flattening; Delambre noted this himself. And Méchain's 3.24″, had it been used, would have moved the metre a quarter (mean) or half (Barcelona) of the way towards the modern value. Delambre's 1810 preference for the combined latitude was the right direction for the wrong reason: the real offset is the deflection at Montjouy, not the gap between the two Barcelona stations.

Vaníček and Foroughi's abstract partitions English Wikipedia, citing Vaníček and Foroughi, partitions the total error as about 95% gravity field (deflection), 3% flattening and under 2% arc length. secondhand That agrees with mine on the dominant term. My flattening term is about −10% of the total; the source gives no sign, and I can't tell how they define the term without the paper. guess

Verdict

my reading Méchain's discrepancy was real in his reductions and he concealed it, but it never entered the metre. What made the metre short was the plumb line at the ends of the arc, which neither astronomer could measure. The flattening was a minor term, and in this arc it pushed the wrong way. The popular story gets both culprits wrong, and Delambre's own diagnosis, irregularities of the Earth, was right.

Still open

  • Méchain's raw zenith distances (vol. II, pp. 503 and 566) re-reduced, to get 3.24″ from scratch.
  • A late passage (vol. III, p. 554) whose three corrections don't quite agree with each other (4.52 − 1.31 = 3.21, and 2.97 + 1.62 = 4.59): rounding or a slip.
  • The deflections are relative to an older datum, and mixing them with the modern flattening is an approximation worth perhaps 0.01 mm. guess
