Drop the needle yourself

Lazzarini's second series, rebuilt: a needle 2.5 cm long, dropped on lines 3 cm apart. It crosses a line with probability 5/(3π), so drops and crossings estimate π. Watch how slowly the estimate settles, and how easily a well-chosen stop lands on 355/113.

What to try

  • Run to 4,000. The line wanders well outside the second decimal for most of the run. At 3,408 drops an honest estimate is typically off by about ±0.05. computed Lazzarini's squares hug the dashed line far more closely than the band allows.
  • Tick "stop at the first 355/113" and run. With some seeds it stops within a few hundred drops. With others, including the starting seed 1901, there is no such row by 4,000: raise "Stop at" to 100,000 and seed 1901 stops at 68,160 drops and 36,160 crossings, k = 320. When it stops on the fraction, the estimate is 3.1415929, wrong only in the seventh decimal.
  • Run many. The button below repeats the experiment and counts how often each way of stopping gives 355/113.

Why 3,408 and 1,808 give 355/113

With a 2.5 cm needle and 3 cm lines the estimate is (2 × 2.5 / 3) × drops / crossings = 5 × drops / (3 × crossings). Put in 3,408 and 1,808: 17,040 / 5,424, and both divide by 48, leaving 355/113. The same fraction comes out whenever drops and crossings are 213k and 113k for a whole number k, since 5 × 213k / (3 × 113k) = 1,065/339 = 355/113, and only then. Lazzarini's row is k = 16. computed

355/113 is the old Chinese approximation to π, 3.1415929…, too big by 2.7×10⁻⁷. The fraction is good. That says nothing about the experiment, because the closeness is a property of the fraction, and the experiment only has to land on its family.

Why landing on it is not accuracy

An estimate's accuracy is how far it would typically be from π if you did the same thing again. Here "the same thing" includes the rule for when to stop.

  • Stop at a number fixed in advance. At 3,408 drops the crossings are 1,808 ± 29 (one standard deviation), so the estimate is π ± 0.05, and exactly 1,808 turns up about 1 time in 73. A seven-decimal match from a fixed stop is a fluke of probability about 0.014, and the next run would be off in the second decimal. computed
  • Stop when the count makes 355/113. Then the estimate is 355/113 every time you stop, whatever π is. Watching after every drop, about 29% of honest runs reach such a row by 3,408 drops. computed A rule that can only ever report one value can't measure anything: run it with a needle of the wrong length and it would still stop on a "good" row, just at different counts.
  • The honest measure of a stop-on-hit run is how many drops it took, and that carries almost no information about π: the number tells you which member of the 213k family you happened to reach first.

So the 3,408 row by itself is best read as a choice of stopping point. How the bench works

  • Each needle gets a centre uniformly placed on the ruled surface and a direction taken from a random point in a disc (draw a point in the square, keep it if it falls inside the circle). The simulation itself never uses π, so it can't quietly assume the answer. It crosses if its two ends lie between different lines.
  • π is used only for the expected count, the band and the error column, which compare against it.
  • The random numbers come from a small seeded generator, so a seed reproduces a run exactly. "Run many" takes its own stream from the same seed.
  • The page's earlier figures (0.0137, 1 in 73, 0.29) come from my exact calculations on the Lazzarini page. "Run many" gives simulated ones, which wander by a percent or so with 2,000 runs.
  • The squares are Lazzarini's printed second series (53, 107, 524, 1,060, 1,591, 1,808 and 2,122 crossings at 100, 200, 1,000, 2,000, 3,000, 3,408 and 4,000 drops), as transcribed by van Maanen and printed by Badger. secondhand
