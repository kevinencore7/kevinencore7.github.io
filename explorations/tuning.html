Why twelve notes?

A piano divides the octave into twelve equal steps. Why twelve, and not 19, 31 or 53? Pick a number of steps and see how far each simple interval lands from its pure, whole-number ratio. Then listen to the difference.

What is being measured

A just interval is a whole-number frequency ratio: 3/2 for the fifth, 5/4 for the major third. An equal division of the octave into n steps can only offer multiples of 1200/n cents, so it has to round. Here each prime, 2, 3, 5 and 7, goes to its nearest step, and every other interval is built from its primes. So 6/5, which is 2 × 3 / 5, gets the steps of 2 plus the steps of 3 minus the steps of 5. Tuning theorists call this the patent val; it keeps a division's intervals adding up the way the ratios do.

The error of an interval is its tempered size minus its just size, in cents. The 5-limit set is 3/2, 5/4 and 6/5; the 7-limit set adds 7/4, 7/5 and 7/6. A set's score is its worst error. Two views compare divisions:

  • In cents. A record is a division whose worst error is smaller than that of every division with fewer steps.
  • As a fraction of a step. More steps always allow smaller errors, so dividing by the step size is fairer between a small division and a large one. A division chosen at random would miss a single interval by a quarter of a step on average.

Other measures exist, with weights for simpler ratios or a sum over all of them instead of the worst. They would move the lists below at the edges. my reading

What to try

  • Start at 12. Its fifth is 1.96 cents flat, 0.020 of a step. Only 41 and 53 do better as a fraction of a step, anywhere up to 72. Its thirds are its weak point: the major third is 13.69 cents sharp and the minor third 15.64 flat. computed
  • Go to 19. The fifth is worse, 7.22 flat, but the major third improves to 7.37 flat. Among divisions from 5 to 72, 19 ranks fourth in the 5-limit by fraction of a step, and 12 sixth. computed
  • Go to 31. The major third is 0.78 cents sharp and the harmonic seventh, 7/4, 1.08 flat. In the 7-limit, by fraction of a step, nothing up to 72 beats it. computed
  • Go to 53. The fifth is 0.07 cents flat and the third 1.41 flat: the best 5-limit division up to 72 by either measure. computed
  • Try 22. It is a 7-limit record in cents, but by fraction of a step it ranks only eighth in the 7-limit and ninth in the 5-limit, and its fifth is 7.14 cents sharp. What sets it apart is structural. In 12, 19 and 31, four fifths come to exactly two octaves and a major third (they are meantone divisions); in 22 they overshoot by a step. computed Not every good division is meantone: 53 also misses by a step, but its step is only 22.6 cents. computed
  • Look beyond the usual list. 34 is a 5-limit record, 65 is second-best in the 5-limit by fraction of a step, and 72 is second in the 7-limit. The usual list of famous divisions is a fair one, but which members it has depends on the measure, and on history. computed
  • Listen. With six harmonics, a mistuned interval beats: in the just triad on middle C, the root's fifth harmonic and the third's fourth coincide, and so do the root's third and the fifth's second. In 12 steps the third's pair beats about 10.4 times a second and the fifth's about 0.9; in 19, 5.6 and 3.3; in 31, 0.6 and 2.3; in 53, 1.1 and 0.03. computed How much of that you notice depends on your ears and speakers. my reading

Why the fifth records are 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 29, 41, 53

A good fifth in n steps means some whole number m of steps with m/n close to log₂(3/2) = 0.58496… The continued fraction of that number, [0; 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 5, …], gives its best rational approximations: 1/1, 1/2, 3/5, 7/12, 24/41, 31/53. Together with the in-between approximations 2/3, 4/7 and 17/29, these are exactly the record divisions for the fifth. computed

So why twelve?

Twelve is the smallest division with a fifth within 2 cents of pure, its thirds are usable if rough, and it fits under the hand on a keyboard. The arithmetic explains why it is a strong candidate. It doesn't explain why it won over 19 or 31, which are better in the 5-limit. That came by its own historical route, through twelve-note meantone and well-tempered keyboards, which I haven't read the sources for. my reading

31 and 53 in the seventeenth century

  • Huygens and 31. In a 1691 letter Christiaan Huygens argued for dividing the octave into 31 equal parts, worked out with logarithms. He says its fifth is larger than that of the usual quarter-comma meantone by 1/110 of a comma, which the ear could not tell. I compute 1/109.9. He gives the minor third as smaller by "3/110 or about 1/37" and the major third as larger by "4/110, or about 1/28"; I get 1/36.6 and 1/27.5. His table of 32 string lengths matches equal division to the unit. He says the 31-division had already been described, and condemned, by Salinas, and that Mersenne rejected it too; he claims only the demonstration by logarithms, saying neither Salinas nor Mersenne could actually divide the octave into 31 equal parts. read computed
  • Holder, Mercator and 53. William Holder's Treatise (1694) reports that Mersenne found "58¼ Comma's, and somewhat more" in an octave, and that Nicholas Mercator, "working by the Logarithms, finds out but 55, and a little more". The octave holds 55.80 syntonic commas, so Mercator was right. Mercator then took 1/53 of the octave as an "Artificial Comma", which Holder says differs from the natural one by about 1/20 of a comma; I get 1/18.9. All 19 interval counts in Holder's table of 53rds match the nearest step of 53. read computed
  • That is why both 55 and 53 turn up in accounts of Mercator: 55.8 is how many true commas fit in an octave, and 53 is a different, better-fitting unit, chosen because 53 steps get both the fifth and the major third nearly right. my reading

How the page works

  • The script computes every division from 1 to 72 when the page loads, with the same rules as the Python that produced my notes. Its numbers agree with that program's printed table for all 72 divisions, to the two decimals printed, and its three record lists and the ranks of 12, 19, 22, 31, 41 and 53 are the same. A separate recomputation at 50 digits gives 12's fifth as −1.9550 cents and 31's thirds and seventh as +0.7831 and −1.0840; the page gives the same. computed
  • Ranks are among the 68 divisions from 5 to 72, by worst error as a fraction of a step.
  • The sound is made in your browser by the Web Audio interface, only after you press a button: three notes, each a sine or the first six harmonics at falling strength, about two seconds long with soft edges. The loudest setting is a quarter of full scale.
