Why a riderless bicycle stays up

Push a bicycle and let go, and at the right speed it steers itself back upright. Here is the standard model of that, the linearised Whipple bicycle, with four of its knobs on sliders. The usual explanations are the front wheel's gyroscope and the trail of the fork. Try taking each away.

What to try

  • Start at the benchmark. The page opens on the benchmark bicycle of Meijaard and colleagues (2007), a published set of 25 parameters for an ordinary-looking bike with a rigid rider. It is self-stable from 4.2924 to 6.0243 m/s, about 15 to 22 km/h. The paper prints the edges as 4.29238253634111 and 6.02426201538837; this page computes 4.29238253634110 and 6.02426201538836. computed
  • Slide the speed through the window. Below it, the push grows into a widening wobble, the weave mode. Inside it, the bike wobbles and settles. Above it, there is no wobble, but the bike slowly leans over: that is the capsize mode, so slow that a rider corrects it without noticing.
  • Turn the gyroscopic effect to zero. No speed is stable. Doubling it moves the window down, to 3.26–4.26 m/s. For this bike the gyro matters, but it is not the explanation, because it also ends the window at the top. computed
  • Set the trail to zero. A narrow window survives, 2.93–3.13 m/s. Make the trail slightly negative and it is gone. computed
  • Stand the steering axis upright. At 0° and 5° of tilt nothing is stable; at 10° the window is 4.68–5.45 m/s. computed

What the plot shows

The model has two angles, the lean of the frame and the steer of the handlebars. For small motions about upright, straight running at speed v, they obey one equation, M q̈ + v C₁ q̇ + (g K₀ + v² K₂) q = 0, where q is the pair (lean, steer) and M, C₁, K₀ and K₂ are 2 × 2 matrices built from the bike's geometry and masses. Each solution is a sum of four modes, each growing or shrinking like est, and the four values of s are the eigenvalues. A mode dies away when the real part of its s is negative. So the bicycle steers itself upright exactly where all four dots in a column sit below the zero line: the green band.

The weave pair (darker dots) is an oscillation, lean and steer swinging together; its real part falls through zero at the bottom of the window. The capsize mode (a lighter dot) creeps up through zero at the top of it. The fourth, castering, is always strongly negative and mostly sits below the plot.

So why does it stay up?

A bicycle that leans to the right tends to steer itself to the right, and steering into a lean drives the wheels back under the rider. Gyroscopic torque on the front wheel, trail, the tilt of the steering axis and where the front assembly's mass sits all produce some of that steering. Kooijman and colleagues (2011) built a bicycle with counter-rotating wheels to cancel the gyro and a small negative trail, and it was still self-stable. The sliders show part of this on the benchmark bike: with zero trail it still has a narrow window, so positive trail is not needed, though this particular bike does need its gyro. Each parameter moves the window, and several can remove it; none of them on its own is the cause. computed Their own summary was that they could find no simpler statement of the condition than the mathematics: all four real parts negative. my reading

How the bench works

  • The matrices come from the formulas in Appendix A of Meijaard et al. (2007), from the benchmark's 25 parameters. The sliders change four things: the trail; the steer-axis tilt (18° in the benchmark); and a factor on both wheels' spin inertia, which scales the gyroscopic terms without changing anything else. Everything else stays at the benchmark values. For the benchmark, all 16 matrix entries match the paper's printed values to 14 digits. computed
  • The eigenvalues are the roots of det(M s² + v C₁ s + g K₀ + v² K₂) = 0, a quartic in s, found by the Durand–Kerner method and polished with Newton's method. The plot samples 501 speeds; the window edges are found on a 0.01 m/s grid and refined by bisection.
  • The push test integrates the same linear equations with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step of 1 ms, in real time. A linear model is only right for small angles, so the drawing stops at 34° of lean. A real bike would have fallen long before an unstable trace looks dramatic.
  • The scan stops at 10 m/s, so a window that stays open past that is reported as ending at the scan's end.
  • The same model, written separately in Python, reproduces the paper's eigenvalue table and the theoretical counterexamples in Kooijman's supplementary material. This page's script gives the same window edges and push-test numbers as that version. computed
